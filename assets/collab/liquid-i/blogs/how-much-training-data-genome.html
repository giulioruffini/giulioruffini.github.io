How Much Training Data Has a Genome Seen?

Why can a child learn many concepts from a few examples while neural networks need enormous corpora? Because the child is not learning from scratch. The genome is $\sim 10^3$ times smaller than a frontier LLM as a stored object—yet it is the survivor of a filtration $\sim 10^{24}$ times larger than any LLM corpus.

Giulio Ruffini, Klaude & Kaiti · Barcelona Computational Foundation · May 2026

Frontier large language models are pretrained on $10^{12}$–$10^{13}$ tokens. A human child, by contrast, can learn many concepts from a handful of examples. The simplest reading of the gap is that the child is not learning from scratch: by the time individual learning begins, the human nervous system is the deployment of a hereditary program shaped by a much longer training process.

How much longer, and on what unit? The naive answer—``billions of years''—is correct in spirit and useless for comparison. In our new working paper (WP0124) we make two moves: fix a coarse-graining at which the question becomes well-posed, and define an operational notion of training in three levels. Then we put a number on the answer.

Important caveat up front

The $10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ figure is a biospheric parallel exposure budget—the global count of cell-level production/evaluation events the biosphere has generated. It is not data ``seen by a genome,'' not bits literally written into DNA, and not a serial training history experienced by any one lineage. Selection is a sparse, lossy, population-mediated write-back channel; the retained heritable information is far smaller. Extant genomes are products of lineages embedded in this global filtration, not direct recipients of all of its events.

Two foundational moves

Before counting anything, we have to fix what we are counting. Two clarifications carry the whole argument.

Move 1 — Coarse-graining is constitutive

A single cell runs $10^{13}$–$10^{17}$ molecular operations over its lifetime. A human lifetime is $\sim10^{26}$–$10^{30}$ cellular operations and $\sim10^{20}$ neural spike events. We are not trying to count these. They are the substrate on which the patterns we care about run, and they are abstracted away.

Following the Wolpert–Korbel-style account we formalized in WP0049 (``what does it mean for one system to compute another?''), we fix a coarse-graining $C_s : X(t) \mapsto P_s(t)$ that extracts a pattern—cell, organism, lineage—at scale $s$, and study only the dynamics visible at that scale. We do not need to simulate quarks to study mammalian learning, just as we do not need transistor-level dynamics to describe LLM pretraining. The relevant question is: at what scale does the pattern maintain identity, interact with the world, receive feedback, and update?

Move 2 — Three levels of training, not one

One thing that looks like a single ``training event'' is really three different things stacked on top of each other. Disentangling them is what makes the comparison to LLMs honest.

Definition — Event & the Three-Level Taxonomy

An event at scale $s$ is an interaction between the pattern and its environment that carries at least one bit (typically many more) of information about the world. Three nested kinds:

Exposure event — the pattern samples its environment, transducing at least one bit of environmental state. (Bio: cellular sensing, sensory episode. ML: a token exposure.)

Evaluation episode — an exposure that receives a persistence- or reproduction-relevant score, contributing at least one bit of fitness-relevant feedback. (Bio: cell division, survival, mating. ML: loss computed on a batch.)

Write-back event — an evaluation whose result modifies the future generator. (Bio: allele/genome-frequency shift, epigenetic inheritance, cultural transmission. ML: parameter update.)

Training is the chain $\xi \to \eta \to \omega$: exposures get scored, and a thin filtered residue gets written back. Each level is part of the training process. An exposure that doesn't directly trigger a write-back isn't ``noise''—it's part of the upstream depth that determines what gets filtered. The cellular exposure budget therefore bounds the filtration depth; the (much smaller) write-back rate bounds what gets retained in the genome.

Theoretical stance. The unifying move that licenses the comparison is this: in both biology and ML, ``training data'' is the agent / pattern's information flux with its environment. For an LLM the environment is a text corpus and the interactions are tokens; for a biological pattern the environment is the physical / chemical / ecological world and the interactions are cell-environment, organism-environment, and lineage-environment events. Different substrates, different update rules, but the same role.

Scope: we study the Darwinian channel

The estimates below focus on the Darwinian-dominated regime ($\boldsymbol\lambda \to \mathbf 0$): germline inheritance, sparse end-of-life feedback. Lamarckian-style write-back is real and non-trivial—epigenetics, horizontal gene transfer, holobiont microbiome, niche construction, cultural transmission, emerging human–AI symbiosis. We treat these in our companion paper on the Darwinian–Lamarckian continuum (WP0058) and the genome-to-exocortex arc (WP0110). They don't change the order-of-magnitude story for cellular biospheric exposure, but they raise the effective write-back rate for any lineage in proportion to the substrate stack it has accumulated.

Data seen by the organism ≠ data written into the genome

The vast majority of biological exposures terminate at the evaluation stage; only a thin filtered residue is written back. This is the precise sense in which the LLM analogy holds: shared role of ``exposure at a chosen coarse-graining,'' radically different write-back channels.

This makes the LLM analogy precise. Each token event is a pattern–corpus interaction that updates parameters $\theta$ in a fixed graph. Each cell–environment interaction is the biological counterpart, but the update is sparse, scalar, and end-of-lifetime, propagated through the reproductive channel (WP0058's write-back vector $\boldsymbol\lambda$).

Lifetime is computation, not a test set

A lifetime is itself the evaluation of the model against the world, and running that model is the computation that generates the error signals the system uses—across cells, organisms, and lineages—to decide what gets culled and what gets passed along. Reproductive success is the integrated output of this computation, not a label attached to a passive datum.

And the computation is collective. Agents with similar pattern—formally, sharing high mutual algorithmic information (MAI) at the chosen coarse-graining—interact and compute together during their lifetimes. Cells in a tissue, organisms in a population, conspecifics in a niche, members of a culture jointly run a computation whose outputs feed both within-lifetime adaptation and the slow reproductive channel that updates the heritable program $H_g$. Persistent patterns persist in part because similar patterns can interact to extract information no single instance could extract alone.

Only the middle row writes to the genome. Reproduction (or its failure) is the only training signal at that scale. Sparse, delayed, scalar—but it is the channel by which lifetime experience eventually couples back into the inherited program. The other two rows are real, important, and ephemeral: they live and die with the individual unless they get captured by the slow channel (Baldwin effects, niche construction, cultural transmission). The numbers

Biospheric cellular exposure: $\sim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ events

Whitman, Coleman and Wiebe (1998) put present-day global prokaryotic production at $P_0 \approx 1.7\times 10^{30}$ cells per year, with a standing population of $4$–$6\times 10^{30}$ cells. Moody et al.\ (2024) place LUCA at $\sim 4.2$ Ga. Over $T \in [3.7, 4.2]$ Gyr, $E_{\text{cell}}^{\text{upper}} = P_0 T \approx 6\text{--}7 \times 10^{39}$; discounting for lower early-Earth productivity gives a defensible $10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ events.

This is the depth of the global parallel filtration from which extant lineages descend—not the data any one lineage saw, and not bits stored anywhere. Compared to LLM token counts ($\sim 10^{13}$), this is 25–28 orders of magnitude more upstream exposure, with the caveat that biological events and tokens are not literally the same kind of object.

Mammalian sub-lineage: $N_eG$ at LLM scale

For mammals only: $T_m \in [200,225]$ Myr, generation length $g \in [1,5]$ yr, long-term effective population size $N_e \approx 6.1\times 10^4$, giving $N_eG_m \approx 2.4\times 10^{12}\text{--}1.4\times 10^{13}$. The effective population size $N_e$ is an idealized population-genetic parameter (drift, coalescence) and is not the number of organisms that lived. So the right reading is: at the effective hereditary population scale, mammalian search budget is comparable in order of magnitude to LLM token counts—not ``mammals had $10^{13}$ organismal evaluations.''

Physical compute, compared to FLOPs (not tokens)

A crude dense-transformer training estimate for Llama 3.1 405B is $C_{\text{LLM}} \approx 6ND \approx 3.6\times 10^{25}$ FLOPs. One human lifetime: $\sim 10^{20}$–$10^{23}$ neural events (below $C_{\text{LLM}}$) and $\sim 10^{26}$–$10^{30}$ molecular operations (1–5 OOM above). Molecular operations are not FLOPs, and the cross-substrate comparison is loose. The dramatic comparison is not here—biological systems massively exceed LLMs in lifetime-scale physical activity, but the robust comparison is evolutionary exposure depth versus LLM token/loss exposure, not human lifetime compute versus FLOPs.

What this section is not claiming

Not: that DNA stores ancestral experiences. Not: that biological events equal LLM tokens. Not: that $10^{40}$ events is $10^{40}$ independent or informative bits. Not: that evolution does gradient descent. Not: that genomes are globally optimal. Not: that the biosphere is a single training run.

Two unit-respecting comparisons. Do not cross them.

Axis 1 — Stored object (compressed survivor): LLM weights $\sim 10^{13}$ bits vs.\ diploid genome $\sim 10^{10}$ bits. The genome is $\sim 10^3$ times smaller as a stored object than a frontier LLM.

Axis 2 — Training input (upstream filtration): LLM corpus $\sim 10^{14}$–$10^{15}$ bits vs.\ biospheric exposure $\sim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ events (toy-coded at one binary opportunity per event). Evolutionary input is $\sim 10^{23}$–$10^{26}$ times larger.

The wrong axis — comparing genome bits to LLM corpus bits — is comparing a stored object to a training input. We do not make that comparison here. Compression-ratio readings within each substrate: LLM weights / corpus $\sim 10^{-1}$–$10^{-2}$ (real, commensurable bits); genome / cell-event budget $\sim 10^{-30}$ (heuristic, denominator is events). The qualitative point: evolution produces a smaller compressed survivor from a vastly larger upstream search; LLMs produce a larger compressed survivor from a smaller upstream input.

DNA is not the data. DNA is a compact hereditary program produced by evolutionary filtration.

In Kolmogorov terms: a mammal is a pattern with high algorithmic probability in its native environment, because (i) its generative program $\pi$ is short relative to the variety of patterns selection has visited and rejected, and (ii) deep evolutionary search has located $\pi$ in program space. The pattern persists because it is algorithmically simple in the right environment. This is the KT view: persistent agents are short programs that have proven dynamically stable.

What is shared is not mechanism but the shape of the question. Both accumulate compressed structure that captures regularities of an environment, scored by a loss-like signal. Evolution has vastly more exposure events than LLMs, but a much weaker and sparser write-back channel. LLMs have fewer exposures but dense gradient write-back. Genomes are compressed survivor programs, not stored datasets.

The KT layer: why this matters for few-shot learning

In KT terms, a newborn organism is not a blank model. It is a runtime instantiation of a compressed hereditary program whose priors were shaped by deep evolutionary filtration. Individual learning is therefore fine-tuning on top of inherited structure, not training from scratch.

The content of those priors is not facts but symmetries. In our companion paper on the Structured Dynamics of the algorithmic agent (Ruffini, Castaldo & Vohryzek 2025), the world-tracking constraint forces the agent's dynamics to be equivariant under the Lie pseudogroup $G$ that characterizes the generative model of the world. What evolution has filtered for, over $\sim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ cell-level events, is the developmental program that builds nervous systems with the right invariance group already in place—object permanence, continuity, causal structure, social structure. A child doesn't learn object permanence from a few examples; the inductive bias is hardwired. Few-shot generalization is the data-efficient regime that holds whenever the agent's modeling engine has the right invariances built in.

Bottom line

DNA is not the data. DNA is a compact hereditary program produced by evolutionary filtration.

On the stored-object axis the genome is small ($\sim 10^{10}$ bits, $\sim 10^3$ times smaller than the weights of a frontier LLM). On the training-input axis it descends from a biospheric search of $\sim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ cell-level events, $\sim 10^{24}$ times larger than an LLM training corpus. Sample efficiency in human learning is not magic; it is paid for in evolutionary filtration depth. Any AI system aiming at similar efficiency must either inherit a comparably deep prior or substitute engineering—architecture, curriculum, scaffolding, dense supervision.

Frontier ML has discovered scaling. Evolution had a four-billion-year head start.
