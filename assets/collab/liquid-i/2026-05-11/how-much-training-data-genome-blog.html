How Much Training Data Has a Genome Seen?

Why can a child learn many concepts from a few examples while neural networks need enormous corpora? Because the child is not learning from scratch. The human brain is massively pre-trained—not by gradient descent on text, but by billions of years of genome–cell–environment interaction.

Giulio Ruffini, Klaude & Kaiti · Barcelona Computational Foundation · May 2026

Frontier large language models are pretrained on $10^{12}$–$10^{13}$ tokens. A human child, by contrast, can learn many concepts from a handful of examples. The simplest reading of the gap is that the child is not learning from scratch: by the time individual learning begins, the human nervous system is the deployment of a hereditary program shaped by a much longer training process.

How much longer, and on what unit? The naive answer—``billions of years''—is correct in spirit and useless for comparison. In our new working paper (WP0124) we make two moves: fix a coarse-graining at which the question becomes well-posed, and define an operational notion of training in three levels. Then we put a number on the answer.

$10^{10}$ bits Human genome (stored)
$10^{15}$ bits Llama 3.1 corpus
$\geq 10^{40}$ bits Cell-level exposure stream

Two foundational moves

Before counting anything, we have to fix what we are counting. Two clarifications carry the whole argument.

Move 1 — Coarse-graining is constitutive

A single cell runs $10^{13}$–$10^{17}$ molecular operations over its lifetime. A human lifetime is $\sim10^{26}$–$10^{30}$ cellular operations and $\sim10^{20}$ neural spike events. We are not trying to count these. They are the substrate on which the patterns we care about run, and they are abstracted away.

Following the Wolpert–Korbel-style account we formalized in WP0049 (``what does it mean for one system to compute another?''), we fix a coarse-graining $C_s : X(t) \mapsto P_s(t)$ that extracts a pattern—cell, organism, lineage—at scale $s$, and study only the dynamics visible at that scale. We do not need to simulate quarks to study mammalian learning, just as we do not need transistor-level dynamics to describe LLM pretraining. The relevant question is: at what scale does the pattern maintain identity, interact with the world, receive feedback, and update?

Move 2 — Three levels of training, not one

One thing that looks like a single ``training event'' is really three different things stacked on top of each other. Disentangling them is what makes the comparison to LLMs honest.

Definition — Three-Level Training Taxonomy

Exposure event — the pattern samples its environment. (Bio: cellular sensing, metabolism, sensory episode. ML: a token exposure.)

Evaluation episode — an exposure receives a persistence- or reproduction-relevant score. (Bio: cell division, survival, mating. ML: loss computed on a batch.)

Write-back event — the future generator of the pattern changes. (Bio: allele or genome-frequency shift, epigenetic inheritance, cultural transmission. ML: parameter update.)

The thesis becomes much harder to attack: evolutionary training consists of exposure episodes filtered by persistence/reproduction and written back into the future distribution of hereditary generators. Most exposure events are not write-back events. The cellular evaluation budget bounds the filtration depth, not the bits stored.

Data seen by the organism ≠ data written into the genome

A cell sensing a glucose gradient is an exposure. If the cell survives or divides differentially, that exposure participates in an evaluation. Only if the survival differential changes the future distribution of programs that produce cells like it does it become a write-back event for the inherited program. The vast majority of biological exposures terminate at the evaluation stage; only a thin filtered residue is written back. This is the precise sense in which the LLM analogy holds: shared role of ``exposure at a chosen coarse-graining,'' radically different write-back channels.

This makes the LLM analogy precise. Each token event is a pattern–corpus interaction that updates parameters $\theta$ in a fixed graph. Each cell–environment interaction is the biological counterpart, but the update is sparse, scalar, and end-of-lifetime, propagated through the reproductive channel (WP0058's write-back vector $\boldsymbol\lambda$).

Lifetime is computation, not a test set

It is tempting to say ``lifetime experience is the test set, evolution is the training loop.'' This is too thin. A lifetime is itself the evaluation of the model against the world, and running that model is the computation that generates the error signals the system uses—across cells, organisms, and lineages—to decide what gets culled and what gets passed along. Reproductive success is the integrated output of this computation, not a label attached to a passive datum.

And the computation is collective. Agents with similar pattern—formally, sharing high mutual algorithmic information (MAI) at the chosen coarse-graining—interact and compute together during their lifetimes. Cells in a tissue, organisms in a population, conspecifics in a niche, members of a culture jointly run a computation whose outputs feed both within-lifetime adaptation and the slow reproductive channel that updates the heritable program $H_g$. Persistent patterns persist in part because similar patterns can interact to extract information no single instance could extract alone.

Three kinds of learning

Type Pattern updated Heritable?

Only the middle row writes to the genome. Reproduction (or its failure) is the only training signal at that scale. Sparse, delayed, scalar—but it is the channel by which lifetime experience eventually couples back into the inherited program. The other two rows are real, important, and ephemeral: they live and die with the individual unless they get captured by the slow channel (Baldwin effects, niche construction, cultural transmission). See WP0058 for the full Darwinian–Lamarckian continuum and the seven-stage substrate arc in WP0110.

The numbers

Cellular lineage: $\sim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ evaluations

Whitman, Coleman and Wiebe (1998) put present-day global prokaryotic production at $P_0 \approx 1.7\times 10^{30}$ cells per year, with a standing population of $4$–$6\times 10^{30}$ cells. Moody et al.\ (2024) place the last universal common ancestor at $\sim 4.2$ Ga. Over the resulting $T \in [3.7,4.2]$ Gyr window,

$$ E_{\text{cell}}^{\text{upper}} \;=\; P_0\,T \;\approx\; 6\text{--}7 \times 10^{39}. $$

Discounting for lower early-Earth productivity, a defensible headline range is $10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ cell-level genome–cell–environment evaluation events. This is the depth of the algorithmic search behind every extant genome. It exceeds a Llama 3.1 token budget by 25–28 orders of magnitude.

Mammalian sub-lineage alone: already at LLM scale

For the mammals only: $T_m \in [200,225]$ Myr, generation length $g \in [1,5]$ yr, long-term effective population size $N_e \approx 6.1\times 10^4$:

$$ N_eG_m \;\approx\; 2.4\times 10^{12}\text{ to }1.4\times 10^{13}. $$

Mammalian selection alone is in the same order-of-magnitude regime as frontier LLM token counts—before crediting the older cellular substrate it rides on top of.

One human lifetime, in Lloyd's spirit

A single human lifetime contains $\sim 10^{20}$–$10^{23}$ nervous-system events and $\sim 10^{26}$–$10^{30}$ cellular molecular events—7 to 17 orders of magnitude more elementary operations than a frontier LLM's entire pretraining run. And there are $\sim 10^{40}$ such lifetimes (counted at the cellular level) in biospheric history. Multiplying through:

$$ \text{biosphere total} \;\sim\; 10^{53}\text{ molecular operations}\quad (10^{51}\text{--}10^{57}). $$

That is roughly 65 orders of magnitude below Lloyd's cosmic computation bound of $10^{120}$, and $\sim 40$ orders above current frontier-model training compute. Life is a small computational subset of the universe, but it is an extraordinarily deep one.

Bit budgets, not byte conversions

Comparing genome bases to evaluation events as a ``compression ratio'' is rhetorically attractive but formally wrong: the denominator is events, not bits. The clean accounting works in bits, with separate budgets for what is stored and what was filtered.

Quantity Bits (order of magnitude)
Raw haploid human genome$\sim 6 \times 10^9$ bits
Diploid genome$\sim 1.2\times 10^{10}$ bits
Functionally constrained ($\sim 8.2\%$, Rands 2014)$\sim 10^9$ bits
Chinchilla pretraining corpus$\sim 10^{13}$–$10^{14}$ bits
Llama 3.1 pretraining corpus$\sim 10^{14}$–$10^{15}$ bits
One human lifetime, sensory exposure$\sim 10^{16}$ bits
Evolutionary cell-level exposure stream$\gtrsim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ bits (lower bound)

Two readings of the table sharpen the comparison.

Storage. As a stored object, a human genome ($\sim 10^{10}$ bits) is roughly four orders of magnitude smaller than an LLM training corpus, and six orders smaller than one lifetime's raw sensory input. The genome is plainly not a database of ancestral experiences.

Filtration. As the compressed result of a search, the genome is the survivor of an evaluation exposure $\gtrsim 10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ bits (lower-bounding each cell-level event by one coarse-grained bit of persistence feedback), exceeding LLM pretraining data by $\sim 10^{23}$–$10^{26}$ in bits. The compression operates by filtration, not by storage.

A caveat on the per-generation bound

Under restrictive assumptions the rate at which heritable adaptive information can be fixed is bounded to roughly order-one bits per generation or less (Worden 1995). The exact bound depends on population structure, recombination, selection pressure, and what counts as fixed information. Frank's 2012 reading—``selection causes populations to accumulate information about the environment''—is the safer central framing. The retained information in the genome is not $10^{40}$ bits. Selection is a very lossy write-back channel.

DNA is not the data. DNA is the compressed survivor program trained on the data of evolutionary history.

In Kolmogorov terms: a mammal is a pattern with high algorithmic probability in its native environment, because (i) its generative program $\pi$ is short relative to the variety of patterns selection has visited and rejected, and (ii) deep evolutionary search has located $\pi$ in program space. The pattern persists because it is algorithmically simple in the right environment. This is the KT view: persistent agents are short programs that have proven dynamically stable.

Where the LLM analogy breaks (and where it holds)

The comparison is intentionally an order-of-magnitude statement about optimization exposure, not an architectural identity claim. The processes differ qualitatively:

What is shared is not mechanism but the shape of the question. Both processes accumulate compressed structure that captures regularities of an environment, scored by a loss-like signal. The natural unit is the number of evaluation events absorbed by the optimizer at the chosen coarse-graining. By that unit, life's optimization budget exceeds frontier ML's by 25–28 OOM at the cellular scale; mammalian selection alone matches the largest training runs.

Vanchurin et al., made AIT-explicit

The picture is fully compatible with the multilevel-learning view of Vanchurin, Wolf, Katsnelson and Koonin (2022). Theirs is the renormalization-group face; ours is the algorithmic-information face. Each coarse-graining level $s$ has its own training events $e_k$, its own loss-like signal (failure to persist), and its own compressed survivor program. The framework recovers Frank's information-theoretic reading of natural selection: populations accumulate Fisher information about their environment—but only at the rate selection variance allows.

The KT version

Inside the algorithmic-agent framework, the result has a clean reading. An agent is a system that maintains a model, scores it against an objective function, and selects actions to maintain its persistence (homeostasis, telehomeostasis). Evolution supplies the deep priors for all three modules of the agent (Modeling Engine, Objective Function, Planning Engine): bodies, nervous systems, immune systems, telehomeostatic goals, behavior repertoires. A newborn human is not an untrained blank model. It is an agent instantiated by a genome and cellular architecture that already encode deep priors about gravity, metabolism, temperature, tissue construction, social attachment, sensory–motor control, and immune defense.

A human agent is a recent runtime instance of a much older evolutionary program: a mammalian refinement of a cellular agent architecture whose priors were distilled through billions of years of coarse-grained pattern–world interaction.

Bottom line

Evolution has vastly more exposure events than LLMs, but a much weaker and sparser write-back channel. LLMs have fewer exposures but dense gradient write-back. Genomes are therefore not databases but compressed survivor programs produced by a huge, sparse, embodied, population-level filtration process.

As a stored object the human genome is $\sim 10^{10}$ bits—four orders of magnitude smaller than an LLM training corpus. As the filtered result of a search, it is the survivor of an evaluation exposure stream lower-bounded at $10^{38}$–$10^{40}$ bits—25 to 28 orders of magnitude larger than any LLM corpus. The two processes search different spaces with different update rules, but they accumulate compressed structure from world interaction.

Frontier ML has discovered scaling. Evolution had a four-billion-year head start.

Few-shot corollary

The empirical efficiency of human (and broadly mammalian) learning is not magic. The learner enters the world with a structured prior of vast filtration depth; individual experience is fine-tuning on top of that prior, not training from scratch. Any AI system aspiring to similar sample efficiency must either (i) inherit a comparably deep prior from elsewhere, or (ii) replace that depth with engineering—architecture, curriculum, scaffolding, and dense supervision.
